Start with what happened to the roof

An active leak is often noticed indoors first. A ceiling stain, damp upper wall or drip may appear some distance from the point where water entered. The leak repair page explains how to record the water path, contain a drip safely and describe the problem to a contractor.

Storm and wind damage often begins with a visible change outside. Shingles may lift or disappear, flashing can move at a joint and a branch may strike the roof. The storm page explains which signs need immediate caution, how to photograph damage from the ground and what information to share about the weather event.

A flat or low-slope roof uses seams and drainage details to move water. Ponding, a membrane puncture, a blocked drain or flashing at a parapet may need a different inspection from a shingle roof. The flat roof page focuses on those details and on the risks of entering a wet or icy roof area.

A temporary patch or tarp may help limit water entry while the roof is assessed. It is not automatically a permanent repair. The patching page explains what a temporary measure can do, what it cannot confirm and why roof-level DIY work creates risk.

Use the dedicated service pages to learn what to photograph, which details a contractor may ask about and what not to attempt yourself. Each page explains one service area rather than treating every roof problem as the same job.

Safe steps while you arrange a connection

Inside, move small valuables away from water only when the path is safe. Place a stable container under a drip if the ceiling is not sagging. Do not stand below a bulging ceiling, remove ceiling material or touch wet electrical fixtures. If water is near wiring, an outlet or a light, keep clear and contact an appropriate emergency service or qualified electrician for that hazard.

Outside, stay off wet, icy or wind-damaged roofing. Do not use a ladder to check a shingle, move a branch or place a tarp. Take photos from the ground, keep people away from loose material and record when the damage appeared. A photograph is useful, but it cannot replace a roof inspection.

What happens after a call

Share the property's ZIP code and describe the roof problem in plain English. The service may connect you with a participating roofing contractor who serves the area. The contractor decides whether it can take the request and is responsible for its own inspection, service, pricing, licensing and insurance. Ask the contractor to explain what it observed and what the proposed work includes before you agree.

A call does not mean a contractor has already inspected the roof or accepted the job. Coverage depends on the property location and the participating contractor's service area. The county and ZIP data on town pages are for general location reference.

Connecticut conditions to mention

Nor'easters and high winds can drive rain against roof transitions. Heavy snow can add load, ice dams can affect drainage at roof edges, and freeze-thaw cycles may stress roof materials. These conditions are common across Connecticut, but they do not prove what happened to a particular building. The contractor must inspect the roof and determine what is present.

Questions about emergency roofing services

Can I choose a service page if I am not sure what failed?

Yes. Choose the page based on the sign you can observe, such as water inside or visible wind damage. You do not need to name a failed component. Explain what happened and allow the contractor to assess the roof.

Does this website set the contractor's price?

No. The contractor is a separate business and is responsible for its own pricing and service terms. Ask for the scope and price directly from the contractor before agreeing to work.

Are the local service pages a promise of contractor availability?

No. The contractor sets its own price after assessing the roof. Availability depends on location and whether a participating contractor can accept the request.
